A Logistic Regression-Based Tennis Match Rating System

This blog post is based on a work I did in Spring 2023 as an analyst with the Cornell Sports Analytics Club.

Introduction

In many sports, player ratings are essential for evaluating individual performance, making roster decisions, and increasing fan engagement. However, existing rating systems in tennis, such as UTR and NTRP, primarily measure long-term ability and do not adequately capture short-term performance. This presents a gap in the evaluation of players, particularly in high-level team tennis, where coaches and players require quantitative analysis to improve.

The goal of this project was to come up with a way to rate tennis performances in a single match, looking at important match statistics, and assigning each player a number between 1 and 10. I utilized a logistic regression model to estimate the probability that a player wins a match based on 32 match statistics, which is then scaled to produce the rating. This approach accomplishes 2 goals:

  1. Provides a quantitative measure of player performance.
  2. Reveals which match statistics are most important in determining the outcome of a match.

The approach looks effective through preliminary testing, although more refinement and testing is needed. Overall, I think it's a novel and useful way of measuring tennis performances.

Motivation

Let's start by defining the difference between rating a player's ability and rating someone's performance. Ability is meant to capture a player’s underlying skill level — how strong they are independent of any single match. It is more stable over time and usually changes slowly as a player improves or declines. Performance, on the other hand, reflects how well a player actually played in a given match or even a specific set. Performance can fluctuate day to day depending on form, fitness, mental state, or external conditions.Measuring these two things should be considered seperate ideas.

To illustrate these differences further, consider a scenario where Carlos Alcaraz plays a match on the ATP tour and a fifteen-year old plays a match in a local tournament on the same day. It is perfectly feasible that Carlos Alcaraz's performance out of 10 is worse than the junior player's, especially if Carlos makes many unforced errors that day and the fifteen-year old plays consistently. However, that fifteen-year old will almost certainly never have the tennis ability of Carlos Alcaraz.

Currently, there are a wide variety of tools to rate the ability of a tennis player. The most popular and effective way is the Universal Tennis Rating, in which competitive tennis players are assigned a number between 1 (novice) and 16 (top professional men's player). There is also NTRP, an outdated system which rates player's abilities from 1 to 7, and a newer system called World Tennis Number Although there is a lack of a good performance rating system in tennis, that does not mean that one would not be valuable. Performance rating systems have value in all sports. For example, in tennis, this could be

  • First and most directly: quantifying the performance of a player in a single match.
  • Comparing performances across matches.
  • Roster management: High school and college coaches can use this to compare players and decide who should play at a certain position.
  • Fan engagement and marketing: for example, the US Open could promote the player with the best performance in the quarterfinal round.
I was inspired by performance rating systems from other sports. In soccer, there is the WhoScored.com rating, in which players are also assigned a number between 1 and 10 for a match. Positive events, like passing a ball that leads to a shot, lead to an increase in rating, whereas negative events, like commiting a foul which leads to a dangerous free kick, lead to lower ratings. With all this in mind, let's establish some principles that a good performance rating system should have.
  1. You should be able to compare ratings across matches.
  2. Ratings should be (almost) independent of the level of a player.
  3. Ratings should be independent of (but probably very correlated to) winning and losing.
Some events, like hitting aces, are indicative of beter play and are more accessible to high level players.

Initially,

Logistic regression models the probability of a binary outcome (win/loss) as: {"$$\\hat{y} = \\sigma(w^T x + b)$$"} where {"$$\\sigma(z) = \\frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}$$"} is the sigmoid function.

To fit the model, I optimized the parameters \(w\) and \(b\) by minimizing the log loss function, which measures how far the predicted probabilities are from the actual match outcomes. The loss for a dataset of \(m\) examples is: {"$$\\mathcal{L}(y, \\hat{y}) = - \\frac{1}{m} \\sum_{i=1}^m \\Big[ y^{(i)} \\log(\\hat{y}^{(i)}) + (1 - y^{(i)}) \\log(1 - \\hat{y}^{(i)}) \\Big]$$"}

Conclusion & Reflections

To evaluate the model, I split the dataset into training and test sets so that the algorithm would be trained on one portion of the matches and then asked to make predictions on matches it had never seen before. For each test example, the model produced a win probability between 0 and 1, which I compared against the true outcome. Predictions above 0.5 were classified as wins, and those below as losses. - basic testing - future testing why i think its bad j - too correlated with winning next steps

While logistic regression models are simple, there are a few drawbacks to this model which must be taken into consideration. First, rating a player by predicting whether they are going to win or not strays slightly from the goal of measuring performance. A player can win despite putting in a poor performance and likewise, they can lose despite putting in a good one. This model will not capture that completely. In addition, many indicators of good play, like depth of shot, average height over the net of a shot, are not directly captured by the model.

Another drawback is the number of statistics a user needs to keep track of to use the model. It is difficult to track over twenty tennis statistics accurately without the help of software which takes point result input. This presents a barrier to large-scale adoption of this model.

Going forward, the model needs additonal testing in tennis matches at a variety of levels. One way to do this is by incorporating this model into match statictic tracking software like this one. This would solve two problems at once: it would lead to easy testing of the model at scale and would make statistic collection feasible.
